### Reading of table 1

- **Bistability is a robust property of the model.** With all weights perturbed by 10%, 97% of the samples stay bistable; with all nAChR currents perturbed by 20%, 88% do. Bistability doesn't depend on a single fine-tuned point.
- **Parameter groups differ a lot in importance.** In decreasing order of influence: tonic drives `I0_*` (only 61% stay bistable at σ = 10%), then weights, then nAChR currents, then adaptation, which has no effect on bistability at all. Perturbing everything at once (54% at σ = 10%) is mostly driven by the tonic drives.
- **Within each group, a few parameters do most of the work** (table 1b):
  - *Control bistability:* `I0_pyr` (bistable only between ×0.89 and ×1.12) and `J_NMDA` (×0.79 – ×1.26) set how close PYR is to the bistable fold. `I0_som`, `I0_vip`, `w_vs` and `w_se` are bounded on one side, and all in the same direction: bistability is lost when SOM inhibition of PYR in the low state becomes too weak. That happens when SOM loses drive (lower `I0_som`), when VIP inhibits SOM more (higher `I0_vip` or `w_vs`), or when the SOM → PYR synapse weakens (lower `w_se`, below ×0.71). Without enough SOM inhibition, the low state disappears.
  - *Shift the low-state rates but keep bistability:* `I_alpha7_som`, `I0_pv`, `w_se`, `g_gaba_base`, `I_alpha5_vip`, `w_vs`, `I_beta2_som` (changes of 50–280% in low-state rates for ±11%, from a 0.45 Hz baseline).
  - *Shift the high state:* only `I0_pyr`, `w_ep` and `J_NMDA` move it by about 10–20%. Everything else changes it by less than 8%.
  - *Almost no effect:* `tau_adapt_som`, `J_adapt_pyr`, `w_pe`, `w_vp`, `w_pp`, `w_ev`, `I_alpha7_pv` (under 10% on any rate). The data don't constrain these parameters.
- **The nAChR currents act only on the low state.** Perturbing them by 20% changes the high state by less than 1% (table 2). In this model, acetylcholine sets the resting regime but not the active one.

### Reading of table 2

- **The high state is robust.** With all weights perturbed by 10%, PYR, PV and VIP high stay within about ±15% of the best fit (10th–90th percentile). With every parameter perturbed by 10%, they stay within about ±20%.
- **SOM high stays at about 0 Hz** (below 1 Hz in every sample, above 0.1 Hz in only 0.3% of them), against a 35 Hz target. In this solution, VIP at about 69 Hz shuts SOM off completely. This is built into the solution, not a local effect: no small perturbation brings SOM back.
- **The low state stays in a physiological range.** For perturbations up to σ = 10%, the 10th–90th percentile ranges are PYR 0–1.9 Hz, SOM 1.2–2.5 Hz, PV 0–2.1 Hz and VIP 0.7–2.0 Hz. The relative changes look large only because the baselines are small (PYR 0.45 Hz).
- **One caveat: the low state can become fully silent.** In 34% of the perturbed samples that stay bistable, PYR low = 0 Hz. A silent low state is qualitatively different from a 0.5–2 Hz resting state, so it is worth reporting.

## 3. Why the low state sits at 0.45 Hz: the Jacobian cap

`bistable_loss` includes `L_jac = relu(max|J| − 5)²`, where J[i, j] = ∂r_i/∂r_j is the effective connectivity at the low fixed point. It **penalises effective connections that are too strong** (|J| > 5). It is not the same as `jacobian_connectivity_penalty` in `loss.py`, which penalises connections that are too weak (|J| < 0.05) in the non-bistable fits.

Several neighbouring parameter sets bring PYR low closer to its 1.75 Hz target. All of them are rejected by this cap, and always because of the same connection, SOM → PYR:

| Point | PYR low (Hz) | J[PYR ← SOM] | L_jac | Total loss |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Best fit | 0.45 | −4.8 | 0 | 4.6 |
| `J_NMDA` ×1.12 | 0.99 | −9.7 | 22 | 39 |
| `g_gaba_base` ×0.79 | 1.41 | −11.5 | 42 | 47 |
| `I_alpha7_som` ×0.89 | 1.72 | −15.9 | 118 | 123 |
| `w_se` ×0.79 | 2.12 | −15.0 | 100 | 105 |

**Interpretation.** In this solution SOM → PYR is by far the strongest synapse (`w_se` = 0.040 nA/Hz, close to its 0.05 upper bound; every other weight is ≤ 0.012). At 0.45 Hz PYR sits near threshold, where its gain is low, so the effective coupling stays just under the cap (−4.8). As soon as PYR fires at 1–2 Hz its gain rises, and the same synapse gives |J| = 10–16: 1 Hz more SOM would remove 10–16 Hz of PYR firing. The cap is therefore what keeps PYR near threshold in the low state.

This suggests that, in this model, a resting state at about 1.75 Hz coexisting with a 54 Hz active state needs **a very strong effective SOM → PYR inhibition**. Two questions follow:
1. Is |J| ≈ 10–16 biologically plausible? If so, the cap of 5 is too strict. Refitting with `--jacobian_weight 0` (or a higher cap) should give PYR low ≈ 1.75 Hz.
2. If not, there may be another solution in which the inhibition holding PYR in the low state is spread over several pathways instead of relying on SOM alone. A refit with a lower `w_se` upper bound would test this.
